Context & Health Status

demography economy NCD burden crisis impact
Chapter summary
Sri Lanka is an island of 21.78 million (Census 2024), multiethnic (Sinhalese 75%, Tamil 15%, Moor 9%), with a rapidly ageing and now shrinking population (growth rate -0.55% in 2024). The country remains lower-middle-income after the 2020 downgrade. Life expectancy has risen to 77.7 years (2024), infant mortality has fallen to 5.3 (2023), and maternal mortality reached a historic low of 25 (2023). But the epidemiological transition has accelerated: NCDs account for 75% of deaths, diabetes prevalence has doubled to 14.6% (STEPS 2021), hypertension affects 34.8% of adults, and overweight/obesity now exceeds 50%. The 2022–2023 economic crisis disrupted medicine supplies and triggered an unprecedented health-worker exodus.

Deep dive · demographic & epidemiological transitions

Population decline. For the first time in modern history, Sri Lanka's population growth turned negative: -0.55% in 2024 (World Bank). The Census 2024 recorded 21,781,800 persons. The inter-census average annual growth rate fell from 0.7% (2001–2012) to 0.5% (2012–2024).

Ageing acceleration. Population over 65 rose from 10.8% (2019) to 12.6% (Census 2024). Those aged 60+ now represent 18.0%. Among those aged 80+, 61% are women — a strongly feminised old age. 45% of those 60+ have at least one NCD.

Mortality achievements. IMR fell from 54.4 (1970) to 5.3 (2023). U5MR from 71.7 to 6.0. MMR reached a historic low of 25 (2023) but has plateaued after rapid decline. Polio, malaria, filariasis, neonatal tetanus and leprosy — eliminated or near-eliminated.

NCD epidemic. According to WHO Health at a Glance (2024), of 148,230 total deaths in 2021, 75% were from NCDs. Cardiovascular diseases alone account for 40%+ of all deaths. GLOBOCAN 2022 recorded 33,243 new cancer cases and 19,145 cancer deaths. Diabetes prevalence has doubled since 2015.

COVID-19 impact. Initial "Zero-COVID" strategy succeeded in 2020 but was abandoned late that year. The Delta wave caused 30,000+ excess deaths. By March 2022, 95% two-dose vaccination coverage was achieved — among the highest in the region.

Emerging burdens. Dengue (89,799 cases in 2023), leptospirosis (9,927 cases in 2023, rising), and TB persist at ~62/100,000 with 4,000–5,000 undetected cases. Chronic kidney disease of unknown aetiology (CKDu) remains concentrated in dry-zone agricultural communities.

Mental health. The economic crisis took a severe toll: 30% of adults reported mental health impacts, and 15% of adolescents considered suicide. Only 1.6% of the health budget is allocated to mental health.

Organization & Governance

structure decentralisation regulation digital health
Chapter summary
The dual preventive/curative system persists under a single DGHS with two parallel hierarchies. The 13th Amendment (1987) framework remains in place but provincial councils have been inactive since 2019 (elections overdue since 2018). Recent developments: the National Digital Health Blueprint was published (Nov 2023), the NMRA gained major new pricing powers (Oct 2025), and the Health Minister was arrested (Feb 2024) over a counterfeit immunoglobulin procurement scandal. A new government (NPP) elected in Sept–Nov 2024 has pledged to raise health spending to 3% of GDP.

Deep dive · regulation, policy & patient empowerment

Legal backbone: Health Services Act 12 of 1952 (revised 1956, 1962). Medical Service Minute No. 662/11 (1991, amended 2001, 2014) governs medical personnel. National Health Policy 2016–2025 addresses UHC, quality, safety, citizen expectations. NSFDHS 2016–2030 extended to 2024.

Key regulators: Sri Lanka Medical Council, Sri Lanka Nursing Council, National Medicines Regulatory Authority (NMRA, est. 2015, now with expanded pricing powers), Private Medical Institutions Regulatory Council (PMIRA/PHSRC), National Authority on Tobacco & Alcohol (NATA, est. 2006).

Patient rights: No explicit Patients' Charter has been enacted. The UHC Policy Analysis Report (Aug 2025) recommended accelerating patient-rights legislation.

Devolution crisis: Provincial council elections have not been held since 2014, making them overdue since 2018. Central government has been managing all health services de facto. The ADB and World Bank have both flagged this as a governance risk.

ADB/WB programs: ADB $200M Results-Based Loan (2025) for Integrated Health Care and UHC; World Bank $150M Primary Healthcare System Enhancing Project (2024–2028) covering all districts.

Health Financing

fiscal crisis tax-funded low fiscal space paying wards
Chapter summary
Government health expenditure collapsed from 2.26% of GDP (2021) to just 1.24% (2023) — a 45% real decline during the economic crisis. Current Health Expenditure (CHE) stands at 3.68% of GDP (2023), but the government's share of general government expenditure fell to 6.33%. Out-of-pocket expenditure declined to 40.22% of CHE (2022), though this partly reflects reduced utilization during the crisis rather than improved financial protection. The new NPP government has pledged to raise health spending to 3% of GDP. Recent innovations include "paying wards" in government hospitals and expanded insurance schemes.

Deep dive · revenue mechanics, crisis impact & recovery

The fiscal collapse. Government health spending fell from 2.26% of GDP (2021) to 1.76% (2022) to 1.24% (2023). The 2023 budget allocated only 1.06% of GDP to health (LKR 322,000 million). The Parliament Sectoral Oversight Committee noted this was "an increase" compared to 1.04% in 2022 — but both figures represent historic lows.

Tax mix remains regressive. Direct taxes made up only ~17% of tax revenue (2018); indirect taxes (VAT, excise, customs) dominate. Revenue collection itself is weak (low tax/GDP ratio), constraining fiscal space even before debt service.

OOPE paradox. OOPE declined from 50%+ to 40.22% of CHE (2022). However, this may reflect reduced utilization during the crisis rather than improved protection. Catastrophic spending remains low (~5.4% at 10% threshold) because the rich pay most OOPE and government covers inpatient care.

IMF program. The $2.9 billion Extended Fund Facility (March 2023) included social protection floor at only 0.6% of GDP — criticized as inadequate. Health sector received $47.2M through the UN Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan.

Provider payment. Public sector remains line-item budget. No DRG, no capitation. No purchaser-provider split. Performance incentives are limited.

The 3% pledge. The NPP government elected in 2024 has pledged to raise government health spending to 3% of GDP. This would more than double current spending but requires significant revenue mobilization.

Physical & Human Resources

hospitals brain drain distribution telemedicine
Chapter summary
The state health network comprises 1,208 institutions with 90,240 beds (~4.0 per 1,000). New infrastructure includes the German-Sri Lanka Friendship Maternity Hospital (411 beds, opened March 2024), Dutch-funded A&E centres in the North, and 13 Chinese loan-funded hospital upgrades. Suwa Seriya now operates 322 ambulances handling 5,000+ calls daily. However, the health workforce faces an existential crisis: 1,489 doctors emigrated in 2022–2024, 25% of government doctors took foreign licensing exams, and 13,880 approved positions remain vacant. Telemedicine guidelines were issued in April 2024.

Deep dive · skill-mix, retention & the emigration crisis

Distribution remains uneven. Western Province concentrates specialists. Rural retention was historically supported by dual practice (allowed since 1977), but the economic crisis has changed the equation.

The brain drain is unprecedented. A peer-reviewed study (Wiley, July 2025) found 1,489 doctors left between 2022–2024. The specialist attrition rate is 6.1% per annum. With 25% of government doctors having completed foreign licensing exams, the threat to the specialist pipeline is existential.

Dual practice under pressure. State-employed doctors may still engage in private practice outside official hours. But with the collapse of the rupee and soaring cost of living, even dual practice income became insufficient — pushing doctors toward full emigration.

PHM (Public Health Midwife) — still the backbone of community MCH delivery, but numbers have fallen from ~7,500 to ~5,746 as recruitment has not kept pace with retirements.

Training pipeline. Eight state university medical faculties produce ~1,500 doctors annually. New faculties at Sabaragamuwa and Moratuwa are expanding capacity, but the emigration rate now exceeds graduation output.

Only 5 child psychiatrists serve the entire country — a critical gap given 15% of adolescents considered suicide during the economic crisis.

Provision of Services

MOH system UHC tracers no gatekeeping HLC network
Chapter summary
Services are within ~4.8 km on average. State covers ~95% of inpatient care and ~50% of outpatient. The UHC Service Coverage Index stands at 67/100 — above the SE Asia average of 62. Maternal and child health indicators remain strong (skilled birth attendance 99%, institutional deliveries 99.9%). Immunization coverage recovered to 99% post-COVID. However, NCD treatment coverage is alarmingly poor: only 36% of hypertensives and 38% of diabetics receive treatment, and among those on treatment, only 39% of hypertensives and 12.4% of diabetics achieve control. Healthy Lifestyle Centres (HLCs) now number ~896 nationwide.

Deep dive · NCD-era service gaps & specific populations

NCD management gap. Healthy Lifestyle Centres (~896) screen 40–65-year-olds — the first such network in South-East Asia, established 2011. But attendance skews female (men work during clinic hours), lab facilities are limited, and follow-up is largely paper-based. The gap between screening and treatment control is the system's weakest link.

Mental health. National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH, 1,409 beds) plus 24 acute psychiatric units in general hospitals. Long ALOS at mental hospitals (~50 days) reflects both real chronicity and structural placement. Only 5 child psychiatrists for the entire country. 15% of adolescents considered suicide during the economic crisis.

Palliative care. Still limited; 11 institutions with consult services; 230 Palliative Health Nursing Officers (PHNOs) provide home care. A proposed community-to-institution model exists but is not yet scaled.

Emergency care. Suwa Seriya (322 ambulances, 5,000+ calls/day, 14:28 min average response) has transformed pre-hospital transport. A&E units at hospitals function 24/7.

Cancer care. GLOBOCAN 2022: 33,243 new cases, 19,145 deaths. Leading cancers: breast (women), lip/oral cavity (men). Cancer hospital ALOS fell from 8.9 to 4.3 days (2004–2017) — improved efficiency but also reflecting case-mix shifts.

Specific populations. Migrant workers, plantation communities, post-conflict Northern/Eastern populations, and prison populations have dedicated programmes but coverage gaps remain. Northern and Eastern provinces are still recovering from conflict legacy.

Principal Health Reforms

timeline UHC pivot shared care cluster post-crisis
Chapter summary
The reform timeline now includes three transformative shock events: the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), the economic collapse (2022–2023), and the NPP political transition (2024) with its pledge to raise health spending to 3% of GDP. The 2018 UHC policy's 5-year action framework concluded in 2024; a comprehensive policy analysis was launched in August 2025. The shared care cluster model is now being scaled with World Bank ($150M) and ADB ($200M) support. Digital health has advanced with the National Digital Health Blueprint (2023) and telemedicine guidelines (2024). NMRA gained landmark pricing powers in October 2025.

The "shared care cluster" — scaling up

Each cluster groups primary medical care units, divisional hospitals, and an "apex" higher hospital. A defined population is assigned. The aim: continuity of care, gatekept referrals, and one electronic record per citizen.

Status: Piloted in 3 districts since 2013 (Polonnaruwa, Nuwara Eliya, Hambantota). ADB HSEP supported 9 districts. WB PHSEP ($150M, 2024–2028) now scaling to 1,000+ PMCIs across ALL districts. The 2025 UHC Policy Analysis confirmed progress but flagged uneven implementation.

The missing reform: fiscal space

Every author of the HiT keeps returning to one point: increased fiscal space for health. The crisis made it worse — government health spending fell to 1.24% of GDP. The NPP government has pledged 3%, which would more than double current spending. Without this, the structural reforms struggle to deliver on quality, equity and chronic-disease management at scale.

International support programs

  • ADB: $200M Results-Based Loan (2025) for Integrated Health Care and UHC Program
  • World Bank: $150M Primary Healthcare System Enhancing Project (2024–2028), all districts
  • WHO: $4.9M emergency appeal (2022); ongoing technical support for UHC
  • UNICEF: Support to 51 hospitals during crisis; immunization recovery
  • IMF: $2.9B EFF (March 2023) — health via social protection floor
  • Direct Relief: $126.9M in medicines donated during crisis

Assessment of the System

UHC equity post-crisis
Chapter summary
Sri Lanka remains the canonical "good health at low cost" case but with a critical new caveat: the health system survived the economic crisis but is now in a fragile recovery. The UHC Service Coverage Index is 67/100 (above SE Asia average of 62). Tax-funded public services, free at point of care, continue delivering strong MCH outcomes. But NCD treatment coverage is critically weak, the brain drain threatens workforce capacity, and government spending at 1.24% of GDP is insufficient for the ageing, NCD-dominant era. The system was built for episodic acute care; the NCD/ageing era demands continuity, gatekeeping and patient-centred PHC — reforms in progress but under-resourced.

Deep dive · efficiency, transparency, accountability

Efficiency. Average LOS is low and falling (1.0–4.0 days by tier). Doctor consultations per capita and hospital discharges per 1,000 remain competitive. But allocative efficiency is a concern: ~40% of government spending sits at tertiary level despite primary care being the NCD priority. Bed occupancy varies 50–80% by tier.

Equity. Per-capita government health spending varies across provinces — Western Province concentrates spending. Northern and Eastern provinces still recovering from conflict. The economic crisis hit all provinces but urban areas recovered faster.

Transparency. Procurement remains a scrutiny point. The Health Minister's arrest (Feb 2024) over counterfeit immunoglobulin highlighted systemic procurement weaknesses. National Audit reports continue to surface periodic issues.

Accountability. Provincial devolution has been inactive since 2019, complicating lines of accountability. The Patients' Charter remains unenacted. Civil society oversight is uneven.

08

Conclusions

synthesisrecovery

What worked — and still works

Tax-funded, publicly-delivered, free-at-point services — driven by early universal franchise (1931), which gave voters the political weight to demand expansion into rural areas. The 1934–35 malaria epidemic embedded two principles still visible today: widely-dispersed services and financial protection. Female education, MOH community delivery, and food/income safety nets did the social heavy-lifting. Together they delivered health outcomes years ahead of income peers. Even through the 2022–2023 economic collapse, the public health system did not collapse — a testament to its resilience.

What now strains the system — new and old

The model was built for episodic acute illness, infectious disease control, and MCH. It now faces ageing, NCDs, mental illness, and citizen expectations rising faster than fiscal space. But the 2022–2023 crisis added three acute stressors: a 45% real decline in government health spending, an unprecedented medical brain drain (1,489 doctors in 2 years), and medicine shortages that put lives at risk. Curative quality has not kept up with preventive achievements. The reform agenda — close-to-client primary care, integrated electronic records, HR planning, demand-side empowerment — is correct on paper but requires more public money and political will to land.

The recurring asks — updated for 2025

Money

Raise public spending from 1.24% to the pledged 3% of GDP; the gap is now critical after the crisis collapse. Broaden fiscal base; preserve OOPE protection.

Structure

Scale shared-care clusters (WB $150M + ADB $200M); enforce gatekeeping; integrate prevention & treatment. Resolve provincial council vacuum.

People

Retain doctors (25% qualified to leave); fill 13,880 vacancies; reform dual practice; expand child psychiatry (currently 5 for 21M people).

Information

Implement National Digital Health Blueprint; achieve interoperability; deploy unique citizen identifier.

NCDs

Close the treatment gap: 64% of hypertensives and 62% of diabetics are untreated. Strengthen HLC follow-up to treatment.

Resilience

Build shock-responsive procurement; diversify medicine supply; protect health workforce from future economic crises.
